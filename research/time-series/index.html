RESEARCH CHAPTER · 01

Time Series Foundation Models

A guided path through temporal modeling: understand the object, establish statistical baselines, learn representations, move to transformers and foundation models, then reconnect the ideas to controlled dynamical systems.

STEP 01

Understand the object

Time series are ordered observations indexed by time. The modeling problem is not only to predict a future value, but to learn structure across scale: trend, seasonality, autocorrelation, regime changes, noise and interactions among variables.

yt = f(yt-1, yt-2, …) + εt
STEP 02

Statistical foundations

Start with transparent assumptions: moving averages, exponential smoothing, autoregression and ARIMA-style models. These provide interpretable baselines before increasing model capacity.

xt = c + Σ φixt-i + εt
STEP 03

Machine learning

Feature-based models can represent nonlinear interactions and heterogeneous covariates. Gradient boosting, tree ensembles and neural networks shift the burden from explicit assumptions toward learned functions.

ŷt+h = fθ(Xt-L:t, Zt-L:t)
STEP 04

Learned representations

Sequence models learn representations that compress useful temporal context. The research question becomes: what information should remain invariant across windows, scales, domains and forecasting horizons?

Context

What temporal window contains the information needed for the task?

Representation

Can a shared embedding support multiple downstream objectives?

Transfer

Can knowledge learned on one collection of series transfer to another?

STEP 05

Transformers

Attention creates content-dependent interactions across tokens or patches. For long sequences, patching and efficient attention become important design choices.

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKT/√dk)V
STEP 06

Foundation models

A foundation-model framing asks whether one pretrained temporal model can support forecasting, classification, anomaly detection, imputation or representation learning across many datasets and domains.

Core question

What does a general-purpose temporal representation need to preserve so that many downstream tasks can be solved without rebuilding the model from scratch?

STEP 07

Controlled dynamical systems

The WAM environments provide controlled trajectories for studying what temporal models actually learn.

Multi-Population Kuramoto

200 coupled phase oscillators organized into five strongly coupled communities.

dθi/dt = ωi + (1/N)ΣjKij sin(θj−θi)

Stuart–Landau Networks

200 nonlinear oscillators organized into seven synchronized clusters.

ż = (λ+iω)z − |z|²z

Kuramoto–Sivashinsky

200 spatial grid points producing chaotic dynamics with an attractor whose Lyapunov dimension is roughly 5–10.

ut + uux + uxx + uxxxx = 0
STEP 08

Low-dimensional structure

A 200-dimensional state representation does not mean the dynamics themselves occupy all 200 degrees of freedom. A chaotic attractor can be embedded in a much smaller effective geometric structure.

DKY = j + (Σi=1j λi)/|λj+1|

For the KS environment described here, the reported attractor dimension is roughly 5–10. This is effective dynamical structure embedded in a higher-dimensional state space—not “200 variables become 5 variables.”

RESEARCH

The frontier

Use controlled trajectories to probe learned representations, vary coupling and initial conditions, measure transfer across regimes, and ask whether a temporal foundation model recovers meaningful dynamical structure rather than only interpolation statistics.
